\editor
\begin{generalcomment}
The manuscript requires a major revision. The main concerns are communication cost in constrained decentralized networks, the gap between the theoretical increasing-gossip schedule and practical constant communication, the role of \algname{}-ii relative to \algname{}-i, and the need for stronger empirical comparisons including adaptive baselines and real-data experiments.
\end{generalcomment}
\begin{revresponse}[We thank the editor and reviewers for the detailed and constructive comments.]
We revised the manuscript and repository to address these concerns directly.
The main changes are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We made the communication model explicit: one gossip round is one local exchange over each directed neighbor relation.  We now count communicated real scalars and distinguish \algname{}-i, which sends $(\mathrm{Wealth},G)$, from \algname{}-ii, DOGD, and the adaptive baselines, which send one $d$-dimensional vector.
    \item We clarified that $q(t)=\order{t}$ is a sufficient schedule for the current proof, not a claim that quadratic communication is necessary or desirable in constrained networks.  The practical experiments emphasize the constrained $q(t)=1$ regime.
    \item We reframed \algname{}-ii as a one-state betting-function formulation rather than an unqualified approximation to \algname{}-i.  We explicitly state the communication/performance trade-off and do not claim that \algname{}-ii uniformly improves over \algname{}-i.
    \item We added decentralized adaptive baselines: AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW, momentum, and Nesterov-type momentum, all with the same gossip interface and communication accounting.
    \item We added real-data review experiments, including bundled real datasets for Docker/offline reproducibility, and preserved the original LIBSVM workflow.
    \item We revised the experimental discussion to avoid overclaiming empirical dominance: tuned adaptive methods can be competitive or better in some short-horizon settings, while the central contribution of \algname{} is learning-rate-free decentralized coin-betting with regret guarantees and explicit communication accounting.
\end{enumerate}
\end{revresponse}

\reviewer
\begin{generalcomment}
The paper proposes a family of parameter-free decentralized online learning algorithms based on coin-betting and a proof of sublinear regret. The reviewer asks for a clearer communication setup, the gossip overhead, a more careful discussion of the centralized gap, and intuition for a real application.
\end{generalcomment}
\begin{revresponse}[We thank the reviewer for identifying the communication-efficiency issues that needed to be stated more concretely.]
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
Although the paper proposes an interesting solution backed by regret guarantees, it is not clear what communication setup is considered or how the approach fits constrained networks.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We revised the manuscript to explicitly describe the communication setup.  The network is an undirected connected graph, implemented through a Metropolis-Hastings gossip matrix, and one gossip round means that each agent exchanges its current communicated state with its immediate neighbors only.
We added the following manuscript text:
\begin{changes}
One gossip round consists of one local exchange over every directed neighbor relation induced by the graph. If $d$ is the model dimension and $|E_{\mathrm{dir}}|$ is the number of directed non-self neighbor transmissions, then one gossip round communicates $|E_{\mathrm{dir}}|d$ real scalars for \algname{}-\pmb{ii}, DOGD, and the adaptive decentralized baselines, and $|E_{\mathrm{dir}}|(d+1)$ real scalars for \algname{}-\pmb{i} because it additionally communicates the scalar wealth.
\end{changes}
This makes clear that the proposed method is local-neighbor decentralized and that the communication cost is now measurable per algorithm and per graph.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
How is gossiping happening? What is the communication overhead? The paper states that $O(t)$ gossiping steps are needed in each round, implying $O(T^2)$ gossiping over the horizon.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We agree that the previous text blurred a sufficient condition for the proof with a practical communication recommendation.
We now state that $q(t)=\order{t}$ is sufficient for the current disagreement proof, but the practical constrained-network regime is constant communication, $q(t)=1$.
For a horizon $T$, constant gossip has cost $\order{T |E_{\mathrm{dir}}| d}$ for one-vector methods and $\order{T |E_{\mathrm{dir}}|(d+1)}$ for \algname{}-i; a linear schedule has $\order{T^2 |E_{\mathrm{dir}}| d}$ cost.
We added code-level accounting of total communicated scalars and report this in the new review experiments.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The claim that a linear schedule closely tracks the centralized oracle is too strong because the figure shows a substantial gap.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We agree.  We revised the discussion to treat the centralized gap as a real finite-horizon decentralization cost rather than as a negligible discrepancy.
The revised manuscript qualifies the interpretation of the communication-regret trade-off as follows:
\begin{changes}
The gap to the centralized oracle in the gossip trade-off figure is a real finite-horizon decentralization cost rather than a claim of equality with the oracle. The linear schedule should be interpreted as a theory-motivated sufficient schedule for controlling the current disagreement bound, not as the recommended operating point for every constrained network.
\end{changes}
Thus, the revised discussion no longer relies on an unqualified ``closely tracks'' interpretation.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
Intuition into how everything is tied to a real application is missing.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We expanded the operational description in the manuscript to connect the algorithm to distributed sensing and collaborative prediction.  Each node makes a local prediction, observes local feedback, updates its coin-betting state, and then gossips only with physical or logical neighbors.  This matches applications such as sensor networks, device-to-device learning, or collaborative streaming regression, where no central coordinator is available and the communication budget is dominated by local neighbor exchanges.
\end{revresponse}

\reviewer
\begin{generalcomment}
The reviewer raises concerns about novelty, theory depth, the theory/practice gap caused by increasing gossip rounds, and limited experiments and baselines.
\end{generalcomment}
\begin{revresponse}[We thank the reviewer for pushing us to sharpen the contribution and empirical claims.]
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The claimed novelty is not sufficiently justified; the method combines coin-betting and gossip and may appear to be a straightforward extension.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We revised the positioning to be more precise.  We no longer frame the contribution as novelty from gossip alone.  Instead, we emphasize three specific contributions: (i) a decentralized online-learning formulation with network regret guarantees for parameter-free coin-betting agents; (ii) an explicit decomposition of regret into average local coin-betting regret and a communication-controlled disagreement term; and (iii) the betting-function formulation underlying \algname{}-ii, which permits a one-state gossiped update and a direct network-regret analysis.
We also expanded the related-work discussion to distinguish our convex online-learning setting from decentralized stochastic/nonconvex parameter-free optimization and from methods that require stepsizes or different problem models.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The theoretical contribution appears limited and the proof techniques resemble existing reward-regret duality and gossip averaging arguments.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We agree that the building blocks are classical, and the revised text is more explicit about this.  The contribution is in how these tools are combined for network regret in a decentralized online setting, and in identifying the disagreement term that must be controlled when coin-betting decisions are driven by gossiped cumulative gradients.  We clarified the proof structure so that readers can see which parts follow standard coin-betting duality and which parts are specific to the decentralized state and betting-function formulation.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The theory relies on increasing communication, while the experiments primarily use constant communication, creating a theory/practice gap.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We agree and now state this explicitly.  The sufficient $q(t)=\order{t}$ schedule is used to prove sublinear disagreement under the current analysis.  However, it is not presented as a practical requirement.  The constant-communication experiments address the constrained-network regime, while the increasing-communication experiment illustrates the theoretical mechanism.  We added text explaining that closing the theory/practice gap for $q(t)=\order{1}$ remains an important theoretical question.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The experimental evaluation is not sufficiently convincing; baselines are limited and the experiments should include adaptive/parameter-free methods, heterogeneous data, larger networks, or adversarial settings.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We added the requested adaptive decentralized baselines: AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW, momentum, and Nesterov-type momentum.  These baselines use the same gossip interface and the same scalar communication accounting as DOGD.
We also added real-data review experiments and preserved the original multi-dataset LIBSVM comparison.  The new runs show that tuned adaptive baselines can be competitive or better on some datasets and horizons; consequently, the revised manuscript no longer claims empirical dominance over all adaptive methods.  Instead, it emphasizes the learning-rate-free property and regret guarantees of \algname{}.
\end{revresponse}

\reviewer
\begin{generalcomment}
The reviewer asks for notation cleanup, a quantitative explanation of \algname{}-ii relative to \algname{}-i, clarification of whether \algname{}-ii satisfies the needed coin-betting condition, discussion of potential-function choices, adaptive baselines, related parameter-free decentralized work, limitations to convex online learning, and fair communication-normalized DECO-i/DECO-ii comparisons.
\end{generalcomment}
\begin{revresponse}[We thank the reviewer for the detailed technical comments.]
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The notation is inconsistent: both $g$ and $c=-g$ are used, and $c$ is overloaded later.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We revised the notation discussion to reduce overloading.  The manuscript now emphasizes $g_{n,t}$ as the subgradient feedback and uses the coin-betting outcome notation only locally when recalling the classical single-agent coin-betting view.  We also avoid reusing $c$ for unrelated constants in the disagreement analysis.  This makes the OCO and coin-betting interpretations easier to follow.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
\algname{}-ii seems to replace $\mathrm{Wealth}_{t-1}$ by $F_{t-1}(G_{t-1})$. The paper should quantify the approximation error and its regret effect.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We clarified that \algname{}-ii should not be read as a heuristic approximation to \algname{}-i with an unquantified error term.  It is a one-state betting-function formulation with update $x_{n,t}=h_t(G_{n,t-1})$.  The analysis for \algname{}-ii is stated directly in terms of the betting function and the disagreement induced by gossiped cumulative gradients.  Empirically, we also acknowledge that \algname{}-i can perform better, and that \algname{}-ii trades off some performance for a smaller communicated state.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
Lemma 3 verifies the condition for \algname{}-i, but the paper should verify whether \algname{}-ii satisfies the same condition.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We revised the explanation around the betting-function formulation to make the proof path for \algname{}-ii explicit.  The \algname{}-ii theorem does not require gossiping wealth; it bounds network regret by combining average local coin-betting regret with a disagreement term controlled by the Lipschitz constants of $h_t$.  The sufficient gossip schedules are then derived for the exponential and KT potentials through those $h_t$-based bounds.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The method removes learning-rate tuning but introduces the choice of potential function and storage of cumulative gradients.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We agree and revised the language to avoid implying that all design choices disappear.  The method is learning-rate-free and horizon/comparator adaptive, but the potential function is still a modeling choice.  We now state this explicitly and report both KT and exponential potentials.  We also clarify that cumulative-gradient storage is one $d$-dimensional vector per agent, comparable to storing optimizer state in adaptive-gradient methods.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The evaluation should include adaptive methods such as AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW, momentum, and Nesterov-type methods.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We added these baselines in the repository and included communication-normalized review experiments.  In the $T=100$ synthetic cycle run, for example, \algname{}-i (KT) had cumulative network loss $27.24$ with $44{,}000$ communicated scalars, while D-RMSProp had loss $20.67$ with $40{,}000$ communicated scalars.  This result is intentionally reported without overclaiming: adaptive baselines can be strong when their hyperparameters are well chosen, whereas \algname{}'s advantage is the absence of learning-rate tuning together with network regret guarantees.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The discussion of prior works on parameter-free decentralized learning is insufficient.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We expanded the related-work positioning to include recent parameter-free decentralized optimization papers, including the works named by the reviewer.  The revised manuscript distinguishes these settings from ours: those works focus on stochastic/nonconvex or composite decentralized optimization and convergence to minimizers, whereas our work addresses convex online learning and network regret against a comparator.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The parameter-free benefit is restricted to convex online learning; many decentralized learning problems are nonconvex.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We added this as an explicit limitation.  Our regret guarantees are for convex online learning (and the associated linearized OLO analysis).  Extending the approach to nonconvex decentralized learning would require a different performance criterion and is outside the scope of the present paper.
\end{revresponse}

\begin{revcomment}
The communication design of \algname{}-i should be justified, and \algname{}-i and \algname{}-ii should be compared under normalized communication cost.
\end{revcomment}
\begin{revresponse}
We now justify \algname{}-i as the direct decentralized form of classical wealth-based coin-betting and \algname{}-ii as the one-state betting-function variant that avoids communicating wealth.  The implementation was revised so \algname{}-ii gossips only $G$; \algname{}-i gossips $(\mathrm{Wealth},G)$.  The new experiments report total communicated scalars.  For example, on the synthetic cycle run with $d=10$, \algname{}-i (KT) used $44{,}000$ scalars and \algname{}-ii (KT) used $40{,}000$ scalars.  This lets the reader attribute performance differences to both update rule and communication budget rather than conflating the two.
\end{revresponse}
